# Early Christian Canon Lists

## Why the lists matter

The Church did not wake up one morning with a sealed table of contents. Bishops, teachers, and local churches named the books they read as Scripture in liturgy and catechesis. Those **canon lists**—catalogues of books treated as normative—are among the clearest historical witnesses to how the biblical canon was *recognized*.

This page gathers the lists that matter most for Catholic study. It is oriented by Edmon L. Gallagher and John D. Meade, *The Biblical Canon Lists from Early Christianity: Texts and Analysis* (Oxford University Press, 2017), a modern scholarly collection of the primary texts. DaKnowledge cites the **ancient sources** themselves; Gallagher and Meade are the conversation partner that helped select and order them. For the finished Catholic teaching on the 73 books, see [The Biblical Canon](canon/).

> *"These are written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God."*  
> — John 20:31

---

## How to read a list

1. **A list is a witness, not always a definition.** Some writers catalogue what their church reads; councils later confirm what the Church receives.
2. **New Testament consensus arrives earlier than Old Testament consensus.** By the late fourth century the twenty-seven NT books are widely stable; debate about several OT books continues longer in the East and among scholars.
3. **"Read in church" and "in the canon" are not always identical labels.** Athanasius, especially, distinguishes books in the canon from books appointed for catechetical reading.
4. **Catholic definition looks to reception.** Hippo, Carthage, and finally Trent settle the Catholic canon; private scholarly hesitation (even Jerome's) does not overturn ecclesial reception.

## Eusebius: three baskets (early 4th c.)

In *Church History* 3.25, Eusebius of Caesarea sorts Christian writings:

| Category | Meaning | Examples he discusses |
|----------|---------|------------------------|
| **Acknowledged** (*homologoumena*) | Universally received | Four Gospels, Acts, Pauline letters, 1 John, 1 Peter |
| **Disputed** (*antilegomena*) | Known to many, questioned by some | James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2–3 John; Revelation in some churches |
| **Rejected** | Spurious or heretical | Works falsely titled or used to support error |

This scheme shows **reception across churches**, not a single early decree inventing Scripture.

## Muratorian Fragment (Western, often ~late 2nd c.)

A Latin fragment published by Muratori lists New Testament books known in the West. The beginning is damaged. It affirms the four Gospels and a large Pauline corpus, and it rejects writings tied to heresy. Dating is debated in scholarship; treat it as an **early Western witness**, not as the final Catholic list.

## Greek witnesses of the fourth century

### St. Cyril of Jerusalem (*Catecheses* 4)

Cyril gives catechumens a careful catalogue of books to read and warns them against apocryphal writings. His list shows the pastoral instinct behind canon talk: protect the flock's reading.

### St. Gregory of Nazianzus and Amphilochius of Iconium

Poetic and iambic catalogues from these Cappadocian-era bishops display strong agreement on the New Testament core, with the kind of local hesitation (especially about Revelation) that Eusebius had already registered.

### St. Athanasius, *Festal Letter* 39 (367)

Athanasius names **exactly the twenty-seven** New Testament books the Catholic Church still receives—the earliest surviving list that matches the NT canon book-for-book. For the Old Testament he reckons a Hebrew-shaped collection as "canonical" and places books such as Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, and Tobit among those **appointed to be read**, especially for those being formed in the faith. That distinction is historically important; it is **not** identical to the later Catholic definition of the 46-book Old Testament. His letter is decisive for the NT and instructive for the vocabulary of "canon."

---

## Latin reception: Jerome, Augustine, Africa

### St. Jerome

Jerome, translator of the Vulgate, prefers the Hebrew Old Testament reckoning in prefaces such as the *Prologus Galeatus* and speaks cautiously about books outside that reckoning. His learning is immense; his preference is **not** the last word of the Latin Church.

### St. Augustine and the African councils

In *On Christian Doctrine* 2.8, Augustine lists the books of Scripture in a shape that matches the Catholic Bible. The councils of **Hippo (393)** and **Carthage (397)** give conciliar expression to that reception for both Testaments—including the deuterocanonical books. The [Council of Trent](../sources/) (1546) later **defined** this received canon in the face of Reformation controversy; it did not invent a novelty.

| Witness | Role for Catholic study |
|---------|-------------------------|
| Jerome | Scholarly Hebrew preference; caution about some books |
| Augustine | Full Catholic-shaped catalogue |
| Hippo / Carthage | Conciliar reception of the 73-book shape |
| Trent, Session IV | Definitive Catholic definition |

## What this means for faith

The lists agree more than popular slogans admit: four Gospels, Pauline letters, and the broad NT core are early and strong. Where the Old Testament lists diverge, the Catholic Church follows the line of **liturgical and conciliar reception** visible in Augustine and the African councils, confirmed at Trent—not the narrowest scholarly subset alone.

> The Church **recognized** the canon; she did not invent it. Scripture and Tradition flow from the same wellspring.

!!! quote "Catechism of the Catholic Church"
    **CCC 120:** *"It was by the apostolic Tradition that the Church discerned which writings are to be included in the list of the sacred books. This complete list is called the canon of Scripture. It includes 46 books for the Old Testament and 27 for the New."*
